> Dominique Guiot, from France is almost completely unknown because he was a library music artist, whose music was recorded specifically for use in media, like movies and television studios and radio stations. Albums like that were never available to the record buying public and was only available to those outfits interested (of course eventually when one of those outfits went out of business or upgraded to CD did those LPs become available to collectors). You probably heard songs coming from such library albums like Alan Tew's "The Big One" being used for The People's Court (the original 1980s run) or "Heavy Action" by Johnny Pearson as heard on Monday Night Football. There were many British labels like KPM Music, Bruton Music and Music de Wolfe that became popular with collectors of library music. Now I am not so familiar with French library music, but Dominique Guiot had a shot but it's hardy like the orchestrated or funky stuff you often associate with library music. In 1978 he released his debut L'Univers de la Mer
> on Chicago 2000 with artwork by Jacques Wyrs (who also did covers for Eloy, Larry Coryell, Ange, Klaus Schulze, etc.) but in 1979 the album received a second pressing on Auvidus with a new, stylized cover with a small excerpt of the original artwork. Unlike many library songs like you've heard on television or radio broadcasts, this music here clearly leans much closer to prog rock with an electronic bent to it. Most of the instruments are done by Dominique himself, including guitar, organ, clavinet, Mini Moog synth and Mellotron, while he gets a guest on sax on one cut. I've seen frequent comparisons to Goblin and Mike Oldfield, but I also can't help but be reminded of the calmer moments of SFF. While many progheads insist on lengthy compositions like Genesis' "Supper's Ready", Dominique proved great prog can be had strictly in a three minute context. There's a nice spacy feel to the music that I really like, like "Wind Surf Ballad", "Les Pingouins S'Amusent", "Destination Inconnue" and "Iceberg en Voyage". "La Danse del Meduses" has a bass line that reminds me of Jean Michel Jarre's "Equinoxe V" but then the Mellotron kicks in and reminds me a bit of King Crimson.
> >
> > Dominique continued to record in the 1980s but those albums are quite different from L'Univers de la Mer. For example, La Dame a la Licorne, released in 1983 on Auvidus, had a more medieval feel to it, with recorders and harpsichord, although synths were still used and the electronic sound still surfaced, but unsurprisingly he used more modern gear and the Mellotron was gone.
